Methods & References ← Back to sandbox

How each process in the sandbox works, the governing equations, the numerical scheme, and the literature used to build it. This is a deliberately simplified, plausible landscape-evolution model — not a research-grade solver — but every process traces to a real method.

Model structure

Elevation lives on a 400×400 grid (50 m cells → 20 km domain) as a float32 field. Each timestep applies, in order: uplift → fluvial incision → hillslope creep (deep-seated failure and glacial erosion are planned). Compute is a CPU reference implementation; the parallel-friendly kernels are structured to port to WebGPU later. See DESIGN.md for the full roadmap.

general mass balance: ∂z/∂t = U − Efluvial − ∇·qhillslope

Uplift U(x,y) is spatially varying, with three patterns: an edge ramp (zero at the rim, ramping to full over a band of cells, then flat — holds the rim as a low base level while the interior rises); a Gaussian centred dome (peak in the middle, smoothly → 0 at the edges); and a tilting plane (linear across the domain, draining off the down-tilt edge). The domain edge is a low-rim base-level outlet, so sediment leaves wherever the interior slopes outward.

1 · Initial topography

Three seeded generators: a tilted plane (control), fractal hills (summed-octave value-noise / fractional Brownian motion), and scattered cones. FBM sums octaves of smoothly interpolated lattice noise with a persistence falloff — the standard procedural-terrain construction.

2 · Hillslope creep (diffusion)

Soil creep is modeled as downslope sediment transport. The linear law makes flux proportional to gradient, giving the classic diffusion equation and convex-up hilltops:

q = −D ∇z  ⟹  ∂z/∂t = D ∇²z

The nonlinear (Roering) law adds a critical slope Sc: flux diverges as gradient approaches Sc, producing planar threshold hillslopes and a natural hand-off to landsliding.

q = −D ∇z / (1 − (|∇z| / Sc)²)

Solved explicitly with a finite-volume flux divergence; the timestep is auto-subdivided to satisfy the diffusion stability limit (D·Δt/Δx² ≲ 0.2).

3 · Flow routing & drainage area

Fluvial incision needs drainage area (how much landscape drains through each cell), which requires routing flow downhill. The pipeline:

  1. Depression filling — priority-flood (+ε) raises pits so every cell has a descending path to a boundary outlet.
  2. D8 receivers — 8-neighbour steepest descent on the filled surface, used for the bed-elevation update and sediment routing.
  3. Topological stack — order cells so receivers precede donors (Braun & Willett's stack), enabling single-pass traversal.
  4. Drainage area, multiple-flow-direction (MFD) — each cell shares its area among all lower neighbours, weighted by slopeP (Holmgren). Single-flow routing forces all flow into one of 8 directions, which on smooth/planar surfaces locks it into parallel grid-aligned stripes that never merge; MFD lets flow spread and reconverge, giving realistic accumulation. The Flow spread control sets P (1 = widest spread, large = steepest-descent-like).

The Flow accumulation shading mode paints this drainage-area field directly (channels in blue, intensity by log area) over a hillshade base, so the network is explicit rather than inferred from relief.

Edge base level: every perimeter cell is forced to sit 1 m below its inward neighbour each step, so the domain edge is always a clean outlet (no spurious edge basins) and base level tracks the evolving interior.

  • Barnes, Lehman & Mulla (2014), Priority-flood depression filling, Computers & Geosciences — doi:10.1016/j.cageo.2013.04.024

4 · Fluvial incision (stream power)

River incision into bedrock follows the stream-power law: erosion rate scales with drainage area A (a proxy for discharge) and channel slope S.

∂z/∂t = U − K · Am · Sn

It is integrated with the Braun & Willett (2013) implicit scheme: marching down the topological stack, each node is updated against its already-updated receiver. For n = 1 this is a closed form; for n ≠ 1 the sandbox uses per-node Newton–Raphson. The scheme is O(n) and unconditionally stable, so one pass handles the whole timestep. As incision balances uplift, relief approaches a steady limit — the 2D analogue of the relief limit in the 1D sandbox.

Sediment transport & deposition — implicit (Yuan et al. 2019): erosion and deposition are solved together in one implicit framework, ∂h/∂t = U − K·Am·Sn + (G/A)·Qs, where Qs is the downstream sediment flux. Each timestep makes one pass to route the flow, then a few Gauss-Seidel iterations down the flow stack: accumulate Qs, then update every cell implicitly against its receiver plus the deposition source G·Qs/A (under-relaxed to tame the deposition feedback). The deposition term turns the net rate depositional as gradient falls — filling valleys and closed basins — while staying unconditionally stable at large timesteps with no sub-stepping. Iterations scale with the deposition coefficient G: G=0 is a single pass (identical to plain implicit stream power, Braun & Willett 2013); G≈1 is ~20 cheap passes — far less work than re-routing every sub-step. The budget is mass-conserving (eroded = deposited + exported), and at equilibrium boundary export balances uplift. The Δelevation and Sediment flux panels visualize this budget directly. This is the method behind fastscapelib.

5 · Glaciers — ice flow, mass balance & avalanching

A glacier-ice layer H sits on the bedrock z; the ice surface is s = z + H. Climate is set by a firn line / equilibrium-line altitude (ELA): the annual mass balance is ḃ = clamp((s − ELA)·β, −ablation, +accumulation) — snow accumulates where the ice surface is above the firn line, ice melts below it (β is the mass-balance gradient). The ablation gradient below the ELA is steeper than the accumulation gradient (≈2.5×, a standard glaciological asymmetry); a symmetric gradient melts too weakly and tongues stretch unrealistically far below the snowline. The balance is referenced to the ice surface, the physical firn line. This carries a genuine surface-elevation feedback — thicker ice raises a colder surface and gains more accumulation — and the resulting small-ice-cap bistability (the ice can sit in a thin or a self-sustaining state); these are real behaviours of the modelled system, not numerical artifacts.

At ice margins the face flux uses an upwind rule: ice can only be transported out of a cell that actually holds ice. A centered face thickness would let an ice-free cell whose bedrock stands above a neighbour's ice surface spuriously source ice into it (the non-negativity clamp then manufactures the mass) — which otherwise stranded phantom patches of ice far below the ELA during deglaciation.

Ice flow uses the Shallow-Ice Approximation (SIA). The depth-integrated flux is driven by the surface slope, q = −D ∇s,   D = Γ·Hn+2·|∇s|n−1,   Γ = 2A/(n+2)·(ρg)n,  n = 3 and thickness evolves by mass conservation ∂H/∂t = ḃ − ∇·q. A is the temperate-ice Glen flow-law coefficient (scaled by an adjustable flow factor standing in for ice temperature / sliding). It is integrated in conservative finite-volume form (fluxes on cell faces, with D = 0 on ice-free faces) so mass is conserved exactly and thickness stays non-negative. At the domain edges the bed is treated as dropping steeply just outside, so ice spills over the rim and leaves the domain — an outlet that gives a bounded equilibrium even when accumulation exceeds the domain's ablation capacity (a closed, no-flux boundary instead lets ice fill the whole grid without limit). The external drop is kept moderate so the edge drains steadily rather than emptying in one step.

Coarse ice grid. The ice runs on a grid 4× coarser than the bedrock (100×100 vs 400×400). Ice is smooth, so sub-grid bed detail is irrelevant; and since the diffusivity cap scales as dx², the coarser grid gives genuine flow speeds (tens–hundreds of m/yr, not throttled) and ~16× fewer cells, making the stiff equilibration cheap. Each step the bed is block-averaged down to the ice grid; ice thickness, speed and erosion are bilinearly interpolated back up to carve the fine bedrock and for display.

SIA diffusion is stiff (D ∝ H⁵), so the flux step is sub-cycled on its CFL limit dt ≤ 0.2·dx²/Dmax (with D capped at a fixed 0.2·dx²/dtsub,min). A semi-implicit ADI solver was prototyped, but because the bed-slope-driven flux is advective it was only stable to dt ≈ 2 yr — no gain.

Equilibrium tracking. Because the landscape evolves over millions of years while ice adjusts in 10²–10³ yr, the ice is effectively always at steady state with the current bed. So rather than advancing ice a fixed time each frame, we iterate the flux + mass balance to equilibrium — until the total ice volume stops changing (a windowed test, robust to the terminus limit-cycle a pointwise test trips on). The first spin-up costs ~10²–10³ sub-steps; afterwards the bed barely moves between frames, so the ice is already near equilibrium and re-converges in ~10² (a per-frame budget caps the cost and spreads large changes, e.g. an ELA shift, over a few frames). A safety thickness cap bounds the unbounded-growth case where accumulation exceeds the ablation capacity of the domain (no steady state exists). Avalanching is folded into the iteration so peaks stay bare at equilibrium.

Avalanching (snow/ice sliding off steep terrain, modelled as debris-limited talus on the ice surface) is currently set aside — the SIA flux already sheds most ice from steep slopes (D ∝ |∇s|²). It may return if bare-peak exposure proves important. The diagnostic panels show ice thickness and depth-averaged ice speed (|q|/H).

Glacial erosion follows the standard landscape-evolution recipe (MacGregor et al. 2000; Egholm et al. 2009; Herman et al. 2015): abrasion proportional to basal sliding, with sliding driven by basal shear stress (Hallet 1979). Taking the erosion exponent l = 1 (Humphrey & Raymond 1994; Cook et al. 2020): ΔE = K_g·τ_b·dt,   τ_b = ρg·H·min(|∇s|, sc) with K_g a glacial erodibility and dt the full geomorphic time-step (carving accumulates over geological time though the ice is quasi-static). The crucial detail is that τ_b is built from the ice-surface slope ∇s: as a trough over-deepens, the ice fills it and the surface flattens, so driving stress falls and erosion shuts off — the overdeepening self-limits to roughly the ice thickness rather than digging without bound (Hergarten 2021; Deal & Prasicek 2021). The slope is Coulomb-regularized (saturates at sc ≈ 0.25 — basal drag cannot exceed a Coulomb yield; Schoof 2005), so a steep wall cannot make τ_b explode, and a realistic per-step ceiling (≲ cm/yr) keeps the bed from out-running the ice's ability to re-flatten its surface. The combination is bounded and stable even at high K_g. Because erosion strengthens with ice thickness, it concentrates under the thick trunk and carves steep-sided U-troughs (with thin-ice walls preserved). Eroded debris is handed to the fluvial system as outwash (next paragraph). Subglacial-hydrology controls on overdeepenings (effective pressure, reverse-slope shutdown) are a refinement.

Fluvial–glacial coupling. Above the firn line precipitation falls as snow (it feeds the ice rather than running off), so rainfall = 0 above the ELA: those cells seed no drainage area, hence no discharge and no river incision in the accumulation zone — fluvial channels begin at the snowline. Bedrock under ice is likewise exempt from subaerial incision.

Lakes. Flow routing is done on the surface (bed + ice), not the bare bedrock, so a glacier is a real barrier: rivers route around it or pond against it rather than passing through. The priority-flood that fills depressions to their outlet is exactly the maximum ponded-water level, so lakes emerge for free as the fill depth (filled − surface) — both ice-dammed / proglacial lakes behind a tongue and ordinary bedrock lakes in closed basins. Lakes are below base level for stream power, so they trap sediment and gradually silt in.

Meltwater & outwash. In the ablation zone the ice wastes, releasing both meltwater and the glacially-ground sediment it carries — each proportional to the local melt rate (the glacier delivers its water and load to where it melts). The meltwater is added to the drainage discharge, so proglacial streams run from the snout; the sediment is injected into the fluvial sediment flux, and the existing deposition machinery spreads it downstream as an outwash plain that tapers with distance. This reuses the fluvial transport solver, so it adds almost no cost. The hand-off is mass-conserving (glacially eroded volume = injected = deposited downstream + exported), with a one-step lag (the glacier feeds the next step's rivers).

6 · Deep-seated landslides (SCOOPS3D-style)

Failure surfaces are trial spherical scoops evaluated with a 3D method of columns limit-equilibrium (Ordinary/Fellenius): each column contributes resisting c·A + (W·cosα − u·A)·tanφ and driving W·sinα. The driving is summed as a vector along the net slide direction, so a symmetric scoop on flat ground (whose radial driving forces cancel) is correctly stable — only an oversteepened, asymmetric mass drives a net downslope failure. The factor of safety is resisting ÷ net driving. Rather than an exhaustive search, testing is stochastic: each step throws a number of random trial scoops — the random draw stands in for a pre-existing weakness, and the FoS check for whether the geometry supports failure. A scoop with FoS below the threshold fails. Controls: cohesion c, friction φ, pore-pressure ratio ru, unit weight, max radius, a slope strength factor (higher = fewer failures; 1.0 = the physical FoS<1 criterion), and tests/step.

Both failure modes share one mechanic: define the slip surface, displace the block downslope along it (a rotation), then resolve over-steepened parts with a growing angle-of-repose relaxation whose region expands to wherever debris lands (so the runout feathers out with no clipped margin). Material can never carve below the pre-failure surface (a per-cell floor), so only the debris flows. All mass-conserving.

Catastrophic uses a large displacement — the block leaves the scar and runs out as a repose-angle lobe. Slow uses a small displacement and the failure becomes a persistent rotational slump: it is stored and re-checked every step — it rides uplift, creeps a little whenever its factor of safety is below threshold, stabilizes as material evacuates (FoS rises), reactivates if later undercut, and is retired once eroded away. Vanish exports the failed mass instead. The two modes carry separate angles of repose (catastrophic debris spreads to a low AOR and runs far; slow slumps keep a steeper AOR and stay compact).

Ice loading (buttressing). Where glaciers overlie the trial surface, each column carries an ice surcharge γ_ice·H_ice·A added to its weight (γ_ice ≈ 9 kN/m³, ~0.45× rock). The surcharge adds to both the driving (W·sinα) and the frictional resistance (W·cosα·tanφ); because the driving is resolved as a vector that largely cancels over a rotational surface while the resistance adds in full, loading a scoop is net stabilizing. So valley/cirque ice buttresses the rock beneath and around it, and deglaciation lowers the factor of safety (paraglacial debuttressing → failures after the ice leaves). The effect is limited by ice's low density — less than half what an equal volume of rock would provide. (This is a surcharge model; true passive support from ice standing in front of a wall is not yet included.)
